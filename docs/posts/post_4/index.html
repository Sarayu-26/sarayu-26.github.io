To: Marin County Planning Department and Board of Supervisors
From: Sarayu Ramnath
Date: November 16, 2025
Subject: Scaling the Bolinas Pilot Septic Program to Strengthen County Coastal Resilience

Executive Summary

This memo recommends that the County of Marin expand the Bolinas Pilot Septic Program into a permanent decentralized wastewater program to strengthen coastal resilience and reduce contamination risk. Bolinas Mesa, a rural neighborhood in Marin County, CA, faces coastal flooding, erosion, and rising groundwater levels that threaten aging septic systems. In this high-groundwater coastal zone, failing systems can release untreated wastewater into the local ecosystem and recreation areas, compromising public health and ecological habitat. The existing Bolinas Pilot Septic Program, which provides limited technical and financial support for upgrading high-risk or unpermitted systems, offers a foundation to address these risks. Expanding the program to cover all high-risk properties on the Mesa would reduce vulnerability and protect the coastal environment.

Problem and Background

Bolinas Mesa, in the unincorporated town of Bolinas in Marin County, hosts aging septic systems in a high-groundwater zone. While Bolinas has a centralized wastewater system, it mainly serves the downtown area, and a moratorium on new sewer connections combined with limited infrastructure capacity means that Mesa residents rely on on-site septic systems. These systems have previously failed after high-impact weather events, causing beach closures and releasing untreated wastewater downstream. The Bolinas Community Public Utility District recently established a special category of Septic System Limited Water Use Permits to support the pilot program, demonstrating local regulatory support. This is an intersecting conservation and public health issue that will be exacerbated if adaptations are not made, highlighting the need for proactive community response and coordinated county support.

Key Findings

1. Risks to Health, Infrastructure & Habitat

Bolinas Mesa faces increasing risks from sea level rise, king tides, and storm events, which raise groundwater levels and increase the likelihood of on-site septic system failures. Past leaks have caused beach closures and water quality advisories, showing direct public health impacts. Because downtown Bolinas sits downhill, septic failures on the Mesa could disrupt critical infrastructure such as the post office, grocery store, gas station, and library. These failures also threaten downstream coastal ecosystems such as Bolinas Lagoon, local beaches, and Duxbury Reef State Marine Conservation Area.

2. Community Equity

Many Mesa residents rely on costly individual systems, disproportionately affecting lower-income households. About one-third of households in Bolinas earn below $50,000, 12.8% of residents live below the poverty line, and 25% of households are renter-occupied, underscoring the importance of accessible support for vulnerable residents.

3. Policy & Funding Frameworks

The Bolinas Pilot Septic Program, led by the Bolinas Community Land Trust, operates with strong local support and partnerships with Marin County permitting authorities, including Bolinas Community Public Utility District (BCPUD) and the County Community Development Agency. The County of Marin has already provided $320,000 in waived permit and road impact fees to support the pilot program. In June 2025, BCPUD passed Resolution 743, establishing a special category of “Septic System Limited Water Use Permits” for the pilot program.

4. Relevant Programs

Coastal and rural regions in California are beginning to address septic vulnerability under sea level rise and groundwater changes. Santa Cruz County’s Local Coastal Program allows exceptions for septic upgrades in rural areas when failing systems pose public health risks. Within Marin County, Stinson Beach Water District and Marin Environmental Health Services are conducting a wastewater feasibility study to explore adaptive approaches. Regionally, the North Coast Resource Partnership has initiated decentralized wastewater planning for rural communities, showing that structured, risk-based programs are becoming more common.

Recommendations

1. Expand Program from Pilot to Permanent

To address the growing risks from aging septic systems and climate-driven impacts, the Bolinas Pilot Septic Program should be scaled up to reach all high-risk properties on the Mesa. Expansion should include on-site upgrades and clustered community systems where feasible, guided by a Mesa-wide, risk-prioritized assessment to identify remaining high-risk systems and evaluate cost-effective options, building on the framework of BCPUD Resolution 743.

2. Align Regulatory Agencies

County of Marin should formalize coordination among Bolinas Community Land Trust, BCPUD, and the County Community Development Agency and Environmental Health Services to streamline permitting, align standards, and provide a clear governance structure. Bolinas Community Land Trust should continue to lead program oversight and participant coordination.

3. Develop a Community Advisory Board

Establish a Mesa Neighborhood Advisory Board to serve as community representatives to Bolinas Community Land Trust, guiding program design, sharing information with residents, and providing feedback on program scaling and implementation. The Advisory Board will ensure transparency, equity in upgrade prioritization, and broad community participation.

4. Leverage State and Federal Funding

The County of Marin should secure and allocate state and federal funds to support program expansion, including the U.S. Department of Agriculture Waste & Water Disposal Loan and Grant Program and the Environmental Protection Agency Clean Water State Revolving Fund. These funds can support system upgrades and repairs, new or clustered system construction, and maintenance infrastructure such as septage treatment works.

Conclusion

Together, these recommendations provide a feasible pathway for County of Marin to expand BCLT’s septic upgrade program from a limited pilot into an established county-aligned initiative. With sustained funding, community engagement, and coordinated regulatory support, the program can reach more unserved households. Bolinas is not the only rural coastal community facing this challenge, and the pilot program is an innovative, community-driven approach to climate-adaptive infrastructure. With targeted County support, the program can strengthen public health protections, safeguard local ecosystems, and provide a replicable framework for rural coastal resilience efforts statewide.

Date: December 9, 2025
To: California Coastal Commission, 45 Fremont Street, Suite 2000, San Francisco, CA 94105
Re: Public Comment on Application No. 9-25-0512 – City of Fort Bragg Desalination Buoy Pilot Project (Hearing Date: December 11, 2025)

Dear Commissioners,

My name is Sarayu Ramnath. I am a lifelong California resident and a graduate student focused on ecosystem-based adaptation solutions. I have both personal and professional experience with our coastal ecosystems, and understand the potential role of innovative water technologies like the Iceberg buoy in advancing sustainable water management in California. However, even small-scale pilot projects must be implemented with rigorous safeguards to ensure the protection of communities that rely on these ecosystems.

I am submitting comments in cautious support of the City of Fort Bragg’s application for a 12-month pilot deployment of the Oneka Desalination Buoy (Iceberg) wave-powered desalination buoy. My support is contingent upon two conditions:

  1. The inclusion of structured, environmental monitoring before and during deployment.
  2. The establishment of a hard-substrate mitigation fund should the buoy or its pipelines disturb sensitive benthic habitat.

Project Context

The Iceberg is a singular wave-powered device that converts seawater to freshwater 0.5 miles offshore in Mill Bay, and delivers it via submerged pipeline to the City of Fort Bragg’s wastewater treatment plant, without using electricity from land-based sources. This single-buoy pilot is expected to produce an average of 13,200 gallons per day over a 12-month period. Preferred deployment areas have been noted as soft-bottom habitat to minimize impacts on marine life, and the pipeline alignment is designed to follow previously disturbed habitat near the wastewater treatment plant’s ocean outfall. This pilot will collect quantitative data that could inform the design and permitting of future utility-scale deployments.

1. Requirement for Comprehensive Monitoring

Although modest in scale, this pilot must include structured, independent, third-party environmental monitoring before, during, and after deployment. Monitoring should track potential impacts to soft-bottom benthic communities, trapping risk for plankton, area usage by foraging marine wildlife, and any unexpected disturbances associated with mooring or pipeline movements. Pre-deployment surveys are essential to establish a baseline to assess project-related effects. Future larger-scale buoy arrays would yield a much larger environmental footprint, which is why empirical data from the pilot should directly inform CEQA review, including if a full Environmental Impact Report (EIR) is needed.

CEQA context: This project is proceeding under an Initial Study/Mitigated Negative Declaration (IS/MND), which evaluates likely impacts and identifies avoidance and minimization measures but does not provide the depth of analysis, alternatives evaluation, or cumulative impact assessment required of a full EIR. Because the pilot will generate the first empirical data on biological, oceanographic, and operational effects of this technology in the state, the monitoring program should be comprehensive enough to inform future permitting.

The IS/MND determined that all potential impacts could be reduced to less-than-significant levels with mitigation, so the pilot would function as a test of those assumptions. Gaps in the IS/MND — including how offshore buoys would interact with migrating wildlife, soft-bottom organisms, or local oceanography — highlight the need for robust environmental review. A publicly transparent monitoring plan, including baseline surveys, would ensure that findings from this pilot meaningfully support long-term policy decisions.

2. Necessity for Hard-Substrate Mitigation Fund

The California Coastal Commission has previously required hard-substrate mitigation fees in similar marine development projects. Although the City plans to site the buoy in soft-substrate habitat to minimize impacts, the environmental setting described in the IS/MND makes clear that the project area is not exclusively soft-substrate. Soft-sediment areas in Fort Bragg’s nearshore waters are closely mixed with rocky reef and kelp forest habitats, supporting some of the region’s highest biodiversity and critical to the recovery of North Coast kelp ecosystems. Recent surveys also document a long-term shift from sand to exposed rocky substrate and the presence of sensitive species such as red abalone, sea urchins, and foliose algae.

Even projects designed for soft-bottom placement can unintentionally impact hard-substrate communities during cable anchoring, pipeline installation, or vessel operations. For precedent, the CCC has applied this mechanism in prior projects, including Southern California Edison’s mitigation for the San Onofre Nuclear Generating Station, which funded the Wheeler North reef — an artificial reef and oversight by CCC scientists to compensate for impacts to the marine environment. Establishing this fund would also allow for adaptive management, supporting ongoing monitoring and mitigation efforts.

If no such fund is yet defined for this project, I request that the Commission commit to establishing one. Although this is a single-buoy pilot, setting up a mitigation fund now will provide accountability for future utility-scale deployments, ensuring that any disturbance, whether incidental or minimal, is offset.

Conclusion

While I recognize that technology like this can play a role in California’s water resilience efforts, environmental protection and monitoring cannot be compromised, even for a pilot project. By ensuring rigorous environmental safeguards in the early stages, the Commission can evaluate water solutions that strengthen community resilience and protect marine ecosystems. I support the deployment of the Iceberg buoy pilot. However, I strongly urge that the Commission require independent, third-party environmental monitoring and establish a hard-substrate mitigation fund to address benthic habitat impacts.

Thank you for considering these comments.

Sincerely,

Sarayu Ramnath Master’s Student (Expected 2026) Bren School of Environmental Science and Management, UC Santa Barbara sramnath@bren.ucsb.edu
